## 1. What problem are we actually solving?

The project is a supervised binary-classification study on the UCI HIGGS benchmark. A row represents one **Monte Carlo-simulated collision event**. The target label identifies the benchmark signal process or the background process. The model receives event-level kinematic quantities and returns a score for the signal class.

This is not a model that observes a Higgs boson directly, aligns quantum particles, simulates the Higgs field, or establishes a particle discovery. The UCI benchmark comes from Baldi, Sadowski and Whiteson (2014): a hypothetical exotic-Higgs signal and a top-quark-pair background can produce the same visible final-state topology. Classification is difficult because the distinction lies in distributions and correlations of event kinematics, not simply in counting visible particles.

The specific benchmark-1 signal studied in the original HIGGS paper is the cascade

`gg → H⁰ → W∓ H± → W∓ W± h⁰ → W∓ W± b b̄`.

The background is gluon-initiated top-pair production, `gg → t t̄ → W⁺b W⁻b̄`. The study uses the semi-leptonic final state `ℓν b j j`: one W decays to an electron or muon plus a neutrino, the other W decays to two jets, and the event contains at least four jets with at least two b-tags. The neutrino is not detected directly; its presence contributes to the missing transverse momentum. Thus the competing processes have the same visible objects but different kinematic distributions and intermediate-resonance structure. This is why the task is a statistical discrimination problem rather than a particle-counting problem.

This signal hypothesis is a **hypothetical heavy/exotic Higgs cascade** that ends in a light Higgs `h⁰ → b b̄`; it should not be conflated with a direct classifier for the 2012 Standard Model Higgs discovery channel. The 2015 follow-up study describes the benchmark data-generation chain as MadGraph5 for hard collision events, PYTHIA for showering/hadronization, and DELPHES for detector-response simulation. The UCI release contains 11 million simulated events and 28 inputs (21 detector-level kinematic variables plus seven derived variables).

The word *quantum* describes the physical theory behind the simulated processes. In quantum field theory, particles are excitations (quanta) of fields; the Higgs boson is an excitation of the Higgs field. The tabular dataset does not contain a representation of each particle's quantum state or a mechanism for physically aligning particles. It contains classical event-level numerical features generated by a simulation and detector-response model.

## 2. Why these 28 features?

The data file has 29 columns: one binary label and 28 inputs. The feature sets deliberately preserve two complementary descriptions of the same event.

### 2.1 Low-level kinematics (21 inputs)

- **Transverse momentum, $p_T$:** momentum perpendicular to the collider beam, $p_T=\sqrt{p_x^2+p_y^2}$. Collider beams travel mainly along the longitudinal axis, so transverse quantities are particularly useful for reconstructing the event's balance.
- **Pseudorapidity, $\eta$:** an angular coordinate, $\eta=-\ln(\tan(\theta/2))$, that expresses direction relative to the beam. It is convenient because differences in pseudorapidity are useful in collider geometry.
- **Azimuth, $\phi$:** the angle around the beam axis. Together, $p_T,\eta,\phi$ describe a particle's momentum direction and transverse scale.
- **Missing transverse energy (MET):** the magnitude and azimuth of the imbalance in visible transverse momentum. Neutrinos interact too weakly to be measured directly in the detector; their presence is inferred through missing transverse momentum, subject to detector and reconstruction effects.
- **Jet variables:** jets are reconstructed sprays of particles produced by quarks or gluons. The jet (p_T,\eta,\phi) describe their kinematics. A **b-tag** is an algorithmic discriminant indicating how compatible a jet is with originating from a bottom quark; it is not a direct observation of a free bottom quark.

These inputs retain relatively direct kinematic information. Their limitation is that a model must learn useful multi-object combinations from the individual coordinates.

### 2.2 High-level derived features (7 inputs)

The high-level variables are composite invariant-mass quantities: $m_{jj},m_{jjj},m_{lv},m_{jlv},m_{bb},m_{wbb},m_{wwbb}$. For a set of reconstructed four-momenta, the invariant mass is calculated (using natural units, $c=1$) as

$
m_{\mathrm{inv}}^2 = (\sum_i E_i)^2 - \left\|\sum_i \vec p_i\right\|^2.
$

The mass is invariant under a Lorentz transformation. A suitable combination can expose a resonance or decay-topology pattern more directly than asking a generic classifier to discover that combination from raw coordinates alone.

These seven values are **expert feature engineering**. They compress selected relationships into physically motivated quantities, but compression can discard directional or fine-grained information. They are deterministic functions of the low-level inputs, not seven independent extra measurements; they inject domain knowledge that may make useful structure easier for a finite-capacity model to learn. The original paper explicitly compares learning from direct low-level kinematics with adding these manually constructed nonlinear summaries. The low-level and high-level sets are therefore compared separately and together:

| Feature set | Input dimension | Scientific question |
|---|---:|---|
| `low_level` | 21 | How much can a model learn from direct event kinematics? |
| `high_level` | 7 | How informative are physicist-derived composite quantities on their own? |
| `all` | 28 | Do the two representations contribute complementary information? |

Do not describe these inputs as individually observed quantum particles being aligned. The model classifies **events** from numerical kinematic descriptors.

## 3. Why these model families?

The experimental design compares models with different inductive biases. The point is not to pick a fashionable algorithm; it is to determine how much benefit comes from a linear boundary, tree interactions or a learned dense representation.

### 3.1 Logistic Regression — transparent baseline

Logistic Regression models the log-odds of the signal class as a linear function of the input features. It is fast, relatively interpretable and provides a useful reference for the value of nonlinear interactions. If its performance is lower than a nonlinear model under the same split and inputs, that is evidence that a linear boundary is insufficient for all the event structure—not evidence that Logistic Regression is inherently a poor classifier.

The implementation places `StandardScaler` inside the scikit-learn pipeline so the scaler is fitted on training data only. The configured inverse regularization strength is $C=1.0$, with L-BFGS and a maximum of 1,000 iterations.

### 3.2 XGBoost — strong tabular nonlinear baseline

XGBoost builds an ensemble of decision trees sequentially, with later trees correcting errors made by earlier ones. Trees can represent nonlinear thresholds and feature interactions without requiring the same input scaling as a linear model. It is a useful comparator for structured tabular data and helps test whether a neural network's gains are specific to learned dense representations.

The XGBoost implementation and configuration must be read from the exact run artifact before reporting a final result. Do not copy a parameter set or training time from another benchmark run.

### 3.3 Compact MLP — learned nonlinear feature interactions

The current compact MLP is a feed-forward neural network. Its configured path is:

$
D \rightarrow 64 \rightarrow 32 \rightarrow 1,
\qquad D\in\{7,21,28\}.
$

Each hidden block applies Linear → BatchNorm1d → ReLU → Dropout(0.20). The output is one logit.

- **Linear layers** learn weighted combinations of the event variables.
- **ReLU** introduces nonlinearity, allowing combinations that a single linear boundary cannot represent.
- **Batch Normalization** normalizes hidden activations within a mini-batch and can make optimization more stable.
- **Dropout (0.20)** randomly masks hidden activations during training, discouraging brittle co-adaptation and helping control overfitting.
- **BCEWithLogitsLoss** combines the sigmoid and binary cross-entropy calculation in a numerically stable form.
- **AdamW** uses adaptive gradient steps and decoupled weight decay; the current configuration uses learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, and batch size 1,024.
- **Early stopping** monitors validation ROC-AUC with patience 5 and restores the best validation checkpoint. This helps avoid selecting a model simply because it trained for more epochs.

The configured network is a compact baseline, not a claim that a deeper or larger model would necessarily be better.

The published HIGGS studies used much larger, carefully optimized networks and much longer training runs than this course implementation. The 2015 follow-up reports optimizing hundreds of architectures and describes an eight-hidden-layer, 300-unit-per-layer tanh network as its best classifier for this benchmark. Our compact two-hidden-layer MLP is a resource-conscious course baseline, not a reproduction of that state-of-the-art configuration; scores from the two setups are not directly comparable.

## 4. Why the split protocol is part of the scientific method

A high score is meaningful only if the evaluation prevents information from the held-out data influencing the model.

The repository's `higgs-split-v1` protocol uses a deterministic stratified 70% / 15% / 15% train, validation and test split (default seed 42). Stratification preserves approximate class proportions in each partition. The same saved row indices must be used for every model in a comparison.

1. **Train (70%):** fit model parameters and any learned preprocessing, such as feature scaling.
2. **Validation (15%):** choose hyperparameters, early-stopping checkpoints and any operating threshold.
3. **Test (15%):** evaluate once after the model and threshold are frozen.

Fitting a scaler before splitting, choosing a threshold after inspecting test labels, allowing duplicate events across partitions, or tuning against the test set can all make a benchmark optimistic. The test set must not be used to train, choose epochs, choose thresholds or repeatedly select the best model.

A deterministic seed aids reproducibility but does not prove robustness. A single seed is not a confidence interval or a systematic-uncertainty estimate. Repeated seeds or bootstrap intervals would be useful extensions if time and compute allow.

## 5. What the evaluation metrics answer

- **ROC-AUC:** how well the score ranks a randomly selected signal event above a background event across thresholds. It is not an accuracy figure and does not select a deployment threshold.
- **PR-AUC / average precision:** summarises precision-recall behaviour. It is especially informative when the positive class is rare; the HIGGS benchmark sample is approximately balanced, so class prevalence must still be stated.
- **Brier score:** mean squared error between a predicted probability and the binary outcome. Lower is better, but a single Brier score does not by itself establish calibration. Reliability diagrams and the score distribution should be reviewed.
- **Accuracy:** fraction correctly classified at one chosen threshold. It can change when the threshold changes and should never replace threshold-independent metrics.
- **Signal efficiency and background rejection:** at a threshold chosen on validation, signal efficiency is the fraction of signal events retained; background rejection is the fraction of background events rejected. Reporting background rejection at a declared signal efficiency is a useful operating-point comparison. It is **not** a discovery significance or a “sigma” result.

The project must label validation and test metrics separately and must state the sample size, feature group, split seed, model configuration and artifact source next to every result.

Until the remaining neural outputs are supplied and the decision owner records a decision:

- keep results labelled by sample size and protocol;
- do not choose a single headline metric;
- do not claim that the MLP is definitively the best model across runs;
- do not treat copied 1M runtime values as per-model training times;
- do not infer calibration or feature-group metadata that the artifact does not record.

A result should be excluded or marked provisional if its feature mapping, sample-selection procedure, split identity or run configuration cannot be established.

## 7. Limits and correct scientific wording

The UCI HIGGS dataset is simulated. It is useful for controlled research into signal/background classification, but simulation does not capture all effects encountered in real detector data, including pile-up, detector noise, dead channels, reconstruction inefficiencies and modelling uncertainties. The sample class balance is also unlike the rare-signal setting of an operating collider.

Recommended description:

> “We benchmark supervised classifiers for signal-versus-background discrimination on Monte Carlo-simulated HIGGS events from the UCI dataset.”

Avoid:

- “Our model detects/discovers the Higgs boson.”
- “We align quantum particles.”
- “The score is the probability that a real event contains a Higgs.”
- “The model achieved a 5-sigma discovery.”
- A single best-model claim that merges the unreconciled 250k and 1M runs.
